Örbit.

A civilisation is more than a flag planted on another planet. How do we create a community that can survive, learn, repair itself and eventually thrive beyond Earth?

01 / The question

What counts as a civilisation?

Örbit shifts the question from “When do humans arrive on Mars?” to “When can a settlement persist without a continuous lifeline from Earth?”

Population & society

Connections before expansion

We use groups of roughly 150 as an organisational design choice, inspired by Dunbar's number — not a hard cognitive law. Three interconnected nodes give the model a starting point for sharing knowledge, responsibility and redundancy.

Systems & survival

Independence is multidimensional

People need dependable energy, water, food, medicine, spare parts, education and governance. Örbit treats these as linked systems. A city of thousands could still be fragile if one irreplaceable component must be imported.

Arrival is not independence

Settlement windows are broad assumptions, not launch schedules: Moon 2040–2090; Mars 2070–2180.

Generations matter

A generation lasts 27 model years. The scenario asks for three locally sustained generations after stability conditions are achieved.

Continuity must survive shocks

Our earlier demographic assumption included a 5% setback per generation. Real-world health, fertility, supply and accident risks require far richer modelling.

The civilisation test: Could Earth stop sending supplies without the Moon and Mars settlements collapsing? Could they preserve knowledge, repair vital systems, support new generations, and found another settlement? Those are Örbit's design goals — not claims that present technology meets them.

07 / Method, limits & reading

Which ideas are established, and which are ours?

What is observed or supported by current engineering work?

Life-support recycling, habitat design, transport architecture and local resource utilisation are active research and engineering topics. NASA's Moon to Mars framework describes a phased approach to exploring these technologies; it does not endorse Örbit's population thresholds, dates or probability outputs.

Is Dunbar's number really a limit of 150 people?

No established universal limit has been demonstrated. The idea inspired our modular community organisation, but the value 150 is best treated here as a design assumption. A 2021 analysis challenged the statistical basis for a fixed value.

What would make Örbit a reproducible scientific model?

Publish source code, a versioned parameters table, random-number seed handling, exact transition rules, definitions of success and failure, output data, uncertainty intervals and validation tests. Then rerun it, test sensitivity and compare against documented evidence. This page deliberately avoids suggesting those steps have already been completed.

Is an independent settlement necessarily a self-sustaining civilisation?

Not necessarily. Independence is gradual and multidimensional. A population could meet a numerical threshold yet still depend on Earth for critical medicine, electronics or replacement parts. Our checklist and long-term test show why population alone is insufficient.
